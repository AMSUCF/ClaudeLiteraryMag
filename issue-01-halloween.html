Fiction

The Last Visitor

Eleanor Thornwood

The house remembers what we choose to forget—every footstep, every whispered prayer, every lie told at the dinner table. My grandmother knew this. She kept a record of visitors in a leather-bound book by the front door, inscribing each name in her careful script: the date, the time, the reason for coming. When she died last October, the book ended mid-sentence.

I inherited the house that November, along with its creaking floors and the scent of lavender that never quite faded from the hallway. The lawyer handed me the keys with a strange look, as if he wanted to say something but thought better of it. I didn't blame him. The Victorian stood alone on Ashwood Lane, a Gothic monument to a family that had dwindled to just me.

The visitor's book sat on the entry table where it had always been, next to the empty crystal bowl that once held peppermints. I opened it that first night, running my fingers over my grandmother's final entry: "October 29, 7:47 PM—" The sentence ended there, the pen leaving a small ink blot where her hand must have stopped.

It was October 29 when I read it. 7:30 PM, to be precise. Seventeen minutes before her final, unfinished record.

I tried to dismiss the coincidence as I unpacked boxes in the parlor, but the house seemed to grow colder as the minutes passed. At 7:45, I found myself drawn back to the entry hall, standing before the visitor's book. The grandfather clock ticked its slow rhythm. Two minutes.

The doorbell rang at exactly 7:47.

Through the frosted glass panel, I could see a silhouette—tall, thin, patient. My hand trembled as I reached for the brass knob. I knew, somehow, that this visitor had been expected. Not by me, but by the house itself, which had been waiting one year for this precise moment.

I opened the door to October wind and swirling leaves. The porch was empty. But on the doormat lay a single sprig of dried lavender, and when I turned back inside, the visitor's book had fallen open. A new line had appeared in my grandmother's handwriting:

"The house has accepted you. You are home."

I picked up the pen and completed what she had started, understanding now that some records are kept not by the living, but by the walls that shelter us. I wrote my own name, the date, the time. And I wrote: "Staying."

The scent of lavender bloomed stronger, and somewhere in the house, a door closed softly, like a sigh of satisfaction.

About the Author: Eleanor Thornwood's fiction has appeared in The Southern Gothic Review, Midnight Gardens, and Apex Magazine. She lives in Charleston, South Carolina, in a house that is only slightly haunted.

Fiction

Autumn Bones

Marcus Ravencroft

Every year on Halloween, the bones emerge from the earth in Millbrook Cemetery. Not all of them—just enough to remind the town of what it tried to bury.

Dr. Sarah Mendoza had dismissed the stories as local legend when she accepted the position as county medical examiner. But now, standing in the cold October dawn among the tilted headstones, she counted seventeen skeletal hands protruding from the ground like grotesque flowers.

"Frost heave," the cemetery caretaker explained, not meeting her eyes. "Happens every autumn. The ground shifts, pushes things up."

"Frost heave doesn't arrange bones in a circle," Sarah replied, gesturing to the pattern before them. The hands formed a perfect ring around a grave marked only "Unknown, 1887."

She'd spent three weeks exhuming and examining the remains. Seventeen bodies, all young women, all showing signs of arsenic poisoning. The town's records from 1887 listed them as victims of influenza. A convenient lie.

The Historical Society fought her investigation. The mayor called her obsessed. But Sarah pressed on, driven by something more than professional duty. She'd started dreaming of the women—seeing their faces, hearing their voices, feeling their rage.

On Halloween Eve, she presented her findings to the town council: Dr. Thaddeus Blackwood, prominent physician and philanthropist, had poisoned seventeen patients who threatened to expose his illegal medical experiments. The town's founding fathers had helped him cover it up, creating the influenza story and burying the women in unmarked graves.

"This happened 138 years ago," the mayor protested. "Why dredge it up now?"

"Because they won't rest until someone tells the truth," Sarah said.

That night, she returned to the cemetery alone and placed seventeen brass nameplates on the graves, each engraved with a woman's name recovered from church records. As she hammered the last one into the ground, the air grew still. The oppressive weight she'd felt for weeks—the weight of angry ghosts—lifted like morning mist.

The next Halloween, no bones emerged from the earth. The ground finally allowed its dead to sleep.

But in the town's oldest archives, seventeen new faces appeared in a photograph dated 1887, smiling now, no longer erased. Some stories demand to be told, even if it takes a century to find someone willing to listen.

About the Author: Marcus Ravencroft teaches forensic anthropology at the University of Vermont. His short fiction explores the intersection of history, science, and the supernatural. This is his first publication in Claude Literary Magazine.

Fiction

The Pumpkin Thief's Confession

Sarah Chen

I steal jack-o'-lanterns. Not for the thrill or the chaos—though there's plenty of both—but because they call to me. Each carved pumpkin is a captured soul, and I'm the only one who can hear them screaming.

It started when I was seven. My neighbor Mrs. Kowalski carved the most beautiful jack-o'-lantern I'd ever seen: a witch on a broomstick, flying across a crescent moon. That night, I woke to crying. Not a child's cry, but something ancient and desperate. I followed the sound to her porch.

The pumpkin was weeping. Actual tears rolling down its orange flesh. When I touched it, I saw her: a woman accused of witchcraft in 1692, burned at the stake for healing the sick with herbs. Her essence had been waiting, searching for a way back into the world, and found it in this carved image.

"Set me free," she whispered. "Please."

I took the pumpkin to the woods and smashed it against a tree. The fragments glowed briefly, then a column of light rose skyward. Her spirit, finally released.

Since then, I've learned to identify them: the haunted jack-o'-lanterns. Most are just pumpkins. But some—carved with particular skill or passion, or by someone grieving—become vessels. They trap fragments of the dead who aren't ready to go.

Last Halloween, I stole forty-seven pumpkins. This year, I've already liberated thirty-two. The police have a task force investigating "the Pumpkin Thief." Social media has turned me into a meme. But tonight, Halloween night, I'll steal my last one.

It's mine. The jack-o'-lantern I carved for my daughter three years ago, the week before she died. I've kept it preserved, watched her spirit become more and more trapped in that cheerful, toothy grin I carved while she giggled beside me.

Tonight, I'll finally let her go. And when I do, I'll stop being the Pumpkin Thief. I'll just be a father who learned to release what he loves most.

The pumpkin sits on my kitchen table, my daughter's spirit glowing faintly inside. She's been waiting for me to be ready. I think I finally am.

After tonight, may all jack-o'-lanterns be just pumpkins again. May all souls find their way home.

About the Author: Sarah Chen is a grief counselor and writer based in Portland, Oregon. Her work explores themes of loss, memory, and the rituals we create to process both. "The Pumpkin Thief's Confession" is dedicated to her daughter, Emma.

Poetry

October Elegy

Diana Whitmore

The year is dying in burnt orange and gold,

a slow decline into darkness, dignified

as any death can be. The trees undress

without shame, letting their summer skin

fall to the ground in rustling waves.

My mother died in October, teaching me

that grief has a color—this particular amber,

this smoke-tinged light that slants through bare branches.

Now every autumn is a memorial,

every falling leaf a small rehearsal.

The dead are closest when the veil grows thin,

they say—as if death were a curtain

and not a wall. But I believe it. In October

I feel her in the wind that scatters leaves,

hear her in the geese crying south.

This is the month that teaches us

to let go beautifully, to transform

our endings into celebrations. The pumpkins

glow like lanterns for the lost. The children

dress as ghosts and knock on doors.

And somewhere between costume and truth,

between memory and presence,

I find her again each year—

my mother, my October, my annual

lesson in how endings can be beautiful.

About the Author: Diana Whitmore is the author of three poetry collections, most recently Seasons of Absence (Copper Canyon Press, 2024). She serves as poetry editor for The Autumn Review and teaches at the Iowa Writers' Workshop.

Poetry

The Anatomist

James Blackwell

In the basement laboratory, 1823,

Dr. Hollis arranges his instruments—

scalpel, saw, forceps bright as morning—

and tries not to think of the body's name.

Better to think of systems: circulatory,

respiratory, the elegant mechanics

of muscle and bone. Better to forget

this was a man who walked to market,

who loved his wife, who died in debt.

The resurrection men brought him last Tuesday,

freshly liberated from consecrated ground.

Ten pounds for a corpse, no questions asked.

Science demands sacrifice, they say.

Progress feeds on the poor.

But tonight, as Hollis makes his first incision,

the body's eyes snap open. Dead eyes,

unseeing, yet somehow accusing.

The doctor's hand trembles. The scalpel

clatters to the table.

In nightmares afterward, he sees them all—

every body he dissected rising up,

their wounds gaping, their mouths asking:

Did we consent to teach you?

Did we choose to serve your knowledge?

The anatomist dies mad, they say,

haunted by the unnamed dead.

His final words, scrawled in his journal:

Forgive me. I learned the body's secrets

but lost my own humanity.

About the Author: James Blackwell's poetry explores medical history, ethics, and the Gothic tradition. His work has appeared in JAMA, The Lancet, and Rattle. He practices emergency medicine in Edinburgh, Scotland.

Poetry

Sugar Skulls & Marigolds

María Soledad

Día de los Muertos, and my grandmother's altar

blooms with marigold petals—

cempasúchil, the flower of the dead,

bright as fire, guiding spirits home.

We arrange the calaveras de azúcar,

each sugar skull decorated with icing swirls,

sequins, foil—death as celebration,

not something to fear but to honor,

to welcome back for one sweet night.

My daughter asks why we feed the dead.

I tell her: pan de muerto, chocolate,

their favorite meals—these are bridges

built from sugar and memory,

invitations spelled in marigold.

My mother's photograph smiles from the altar,

surrounded by the things she loved:

her recipe for mole, her rosary,

the shell she found in Veracruz.

Death took her body but not her presence.

This is how we hold them: in ritual,

in the stories we tell, in the sweetness

we offer. The candles flicker

and I swear I smell her perfume—

roses and cinnamon, memory made tangible.

My daughter places a marigold petal

on her grandmother's photo. Death,

I tell her, is not an ending but a doorway.

Love is the key that opens it,

again and again, each November.

About the Author: María Soledad is a Chicana poet from San Antonio, Texas. Her debut collection, Altars of the Living, won the 2024 Andrés Montoya Poetry Prize. She teaches creative writing at Our Lady of the Lake University.

Poetry

Suburban Gothic

Robert Mills

The houses on Elm Street are all the same:

colonial facades, two-car garages,

lawns trimmed to regulation length.

But something moves in the windows

when the streetlights flicker on.

Mrs. Patterson's garden gnomes

rearrange themselves at night.

The Hendersons' sprinkler system

activates at 3 AM, spelling out

words no one wants to read.

We pretend not to notice. We wave

at neighbors who've been dead for months

but still mow their lawns every Saturday.

We attend block parties where the punch

tastes faintly of formaldehyde.

This is the American dream:

property values stable, schools rated highly,

low crime (if you don't count the disappearances).

The realtor never mentioned the previous owners

or why they left in such a hurry.

On Halloween, the children trick-or-treat

past houses that shouldn't exist,

down streets that aren't on any map.

They return with candy that tastes of ash

and memories that aren't their own.

Welcome to the suburbs, where the horror

isn't the haunting but the pretending—

the carefully maintained illusion

that everything is normal, has always been normal,

will always be normal. Forever.

About the Author: Robert Mills grew up in a planned community in Arizona and has never quite recovered. His poetry has appeared in The New Yorker, Poetry Magazine, and The Paris Review. He currently lives in Brooklyn, where the horror is more honest.

Creative Nonfiction

Inheriting Ghosts: A Memoir of My Grandmother's House

Patricia Ng

My grandmother's house in Chinatown smelled of incense and preserved plums, of mothballs and the particular mustiness that accumulates in rooms that haven't been opened in decades. When she died at ninety-three, I inherited not just the house but its ghosts—both literal and metaphorical.

In Chinese culture, we don't fear ghosts the way Americans do. We make space for them. My grandmother kept a small altar in the corner of her kitchen where she burned incense for her parents, her husband, her sister who died young. She left offerings of fruit and tea, updating them weekly like some people change flowers in a vase.

"The dead need taking care of," she told me once in Cantonese. "Just like the living."

I didn't believe in ghosts then. I was twenty-five, newly graduated from Berkeley, armed with a degree in computer science and a Silicon Valley job offer. Ghosts were superstition, relics of an old world I'd been taught to transcend.

But the first night I slept in her house, I heard footsteps. Not creaking floorboards or settling foundations—deliberate footsteps, pacing the hallway outside my room. I opened the door to an empty corridor, but the scent of Tiger Balm hung in the air, the same ointment my grandmother rubbed on her arthritic knees.

In the morning, I found the kitchen altar freshly tended. New incense burned in the holder. A cup of tea, still warm, sat before my grandfather's photograph.

I lived in that house for six months while settling her estate, and slowly I began to understand. The ghosts weren't haunting me—they were family. My grandmother's presence in the creak of her rocking chair. My grandfather's in the way doors closed gently, never slammed. The sister I never met, whose laughter I sometimes heard from the garden where she used to tend roses.

I learned to maintain the altar. At first, it felt like playing pretend, but routine became ritual, and ritual became belief. I burned incense every morning. I left offerings of fruit, of tea, of the almond cookies my grandmother loved. I spoke to them in broken Cantonese, apologizing for my American accent, for how much I'd forgotten.

When I finally sold the house—I couldn't afford to keep it, couldn't bear to watch developers turn it into luxury condos—I performed the ritual my grandmother had taught me for moving the dead. I collected ash from the altar, photographs, personal items. I found them a place in my apartment, a corner where I rebuilt the altar smaller but just as sacred.

The ghosts came with me. I know because my apartment now smells faintly of incense and preserved plums. Because I wake to find tea cups I don't remember filling. Because I'm no longer alone in the particular way immigrants' children are alone, severed from ancestry, floating between cultures.

My American friends think I'm grieving strangely. They suggest therapy, meditation apps, moving on. But I'm not grieving. I'm inheriting. Not just a house, not just objects, but the knowledge that the dead don't leave us. They transform into memory, into ritual, into the stories we tell and retell until we become the bridge between their world and ours.

On Halloween—a holiday my grandmother never celebrated—I think about American ghost stories. The haunted houses, the malevolent spirits, the exorcisms. How different from the Chinese understanding: that ghosts are just family members in a different form, still needing care, still deserving honor.

I tend my altar. I burn my incense. I pour my tea for the dead.

And when I die, I hope my children—if I have them—will do the same for me. I hope they'll inherit not just my possessions but my ghost, and they'll understand that haunting can be an act of love.

About the Author: Patricia Ng writes about Asian-American identity, family, and the spaces between cultures. Her essays have appeared in Catapult, The Rumpus, and Guernica. She lives in San Francisco, where she maintains her grandmother's altar.

Creative Nonfiction

The Fear Harvest: On Working at a Haunted House

Tyler Kowalski

For three Octobers in my early twenties, I worked at Nightmare Manor, a high-end haunted attraction in rural Pennsylvania. My job was simple: terrify people. I wore a burlap mask, carried a fake chainsaw, and chased screaming teenagers through a converted barn. I was good at it. Great, even. I won "Scariest Actor" two years running.

But this isn't a story about scaring people. It's about what I learned by watching people choose to be afraid.

We opened every Friday and Saturday in October, plus Halloween night. Each evening, hundreds of people paid thirty dollars for the privilege of being chased through darkness by strangers in masks. They waited in lines that stretched across the parking lot. They signed waivers. They trembled before they even entered.

Why? That's what I couldn't understand at first. Why would anyone pay for fear?

My manager, a theatrical lighting designer named Marcus who ran the Manor during his off-season, had a theory. "People are afraid all the time," he told me during a slow night. "Afraid of losing their jobs, their health, their loved ones. Real fear that never goes away. But here?" He gestured at our elaborate set. "Here the fear has a time limit. It ends when they walk out. That's what they're paying for—fear with an expiration date."

I started watching the guests differently after that. I noticed the couples who held hands through the entire attraction, testing their relationships against manufactured horror. The teenagers proving their bravery to each other. The parents introducing their children to controlled fear, teaching them that scary things can be survived.

I noticed who screamed and who laughed. Who ran and who stood their ground. Who protected others and who pushed friends toward danger. A haunted house, I realized, was a laboratory of human nature.

The most surprising guests were the ones who came alone. They were rare but memorable. I asked one—a middle-aged woman who'd come through three times in one night—why she kept returning. She considered the question seriously.

"My husband died last year," she said. "Cancer. I spent months watching him suffer, being afraid every day that he'd die, then being afraid of living without him. Your haunted house is the first time in a year that I've felt a different kind of fear. It's almost a relief."

After that conversation, I started seeing the Manor differently. We weren't just providing entertainment. We were offering catharsis. A space where fear could be acknowledged, experienced, and released. Where people could scream without judgment, run without shame, face their dread in a context that promised safety.

There's an art to haunted house acting that few people understand. It's not about being as scary as possible—it's about reading each group and calibrating the fear to what they can handle. The giggling teens get full intensity: chainsaw revving, aggressive chase. The nervous couple gets atmosphere and suggestion. The family with young kids gets spectacle without true menace.

The best actors are empaths. We sense the line between exciting fear and traumatic fear, and we dance right up to it without crossing over. We're fear sommeliers, offering precisely calibrated terror.

I stopped working at Nightmare Manor when I started graduate school for psychology. But those three Octobers taught me more about human nature than any textbook. I learned that fear is complex—sometimes burden, sometimes release, sometimes teacher. I learned that people need safe spaces to confront their dread, to practice courage, to scream and laugh and survive.

Now, when October comes and I pass haunted attractions, I see them differently than my peers do. Not as cheap thrills or Halloween kitsch, but as necessary spaces in our emotional landscape. Places where we harvest our fears, examine them in controlled light, and discover we're braver than we thought.

The real haunted house isn't the converted barn with fake blood and strobe lights. It's the world outside, where fear has no expiration date, where monsters are real and can't be escaped by walking through an exit door. That's the true horror.

But for ninety dollars and forty-five minutes, we pretend otherwise. We let ourselves be chased by actors in masks. We scream and laugh and clutch our friends. We practice being afraid and surviving it.

And maybe, just maybe, that practice helps when we face the real darkness. The fears we can't pay to escape.

That's the gift of the haunted house: a reminder that fear can be survived, that darkness eventually ends, that we're capable of walking through horror and emerging on the other side, still standing, still breathing, still alive.

Still ready for whatever comes next.

About the Author: Tyler Kowalski is a clinical psychologist specializing in anxiety disorders. He writes about fear, courage, and the strange spaces where they intersect. His work has appeared in The Atlantic, Psychology Today, and Creative Nonfiction. He still occasionally works Halloween nights at Nightmare Manor, for old times' sake.
